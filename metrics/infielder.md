## Infielder: Performance Measurements and Position Requirements

For Baseball infielders (shortstop, second baseman, and third baseman), these measurements can help assess speed, power, throwing ability, and athleticism. Infielders place particular emphasis on acceleration, lateral quickness, agility, and explosive power. Targets are **illustrative high-performance reference ranges**, not official MLB requirements. Results vary with age, sex, competition level, testing protocol, and playing role. Youth players should be compared with age-appropriate standards.

## 1. Physical measurements

| Measurement | What it measures | Example high-performance reference |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Statcast sprint speed | Peak running speed during competitive plays | 8.3–9.0+ m/s (30–32.4+ km/h) |
| 10-yard sprint | Initial acceleration | 1.55–1.70 sec (over 9.14 m) |
| 30-yard sprint | Short-distance acceleration and speed | 3.9–4.2 sec (over 27.43 m) |
| 60-yard dash | Overall short-distance running speed | 6.5–6.9 sec (over 54.86 m) |
| 5-10-5 shuttle | Lateral quickness, change of direction, and agility | 4.0–4.4 sec |
| Pro agility / lateral shuffle | Defensive range and lateral movement | 4.0–4.5 sec |
| Throwing velocity | Arm strength, measured with a radar gun | 135–153+ km/h (84–95+ mph) |
| Exit velocity | Speed of the ball off the bat | 145–169+ km/h (90–105+ mph) |
| Bat speed | Speed of the bat through the hitting zone | 105–129+ km/h (65–80+ mph) |
| Vertical jump | Lower-body explosive power | 65–86+ cm (26–34+ in) |
| Standing broad jump | Horizontal explosive power | 2.5–3.1 m |
| Rotational medicine ball throw | Rotational power and core explosiveness | 6–10+ m, depending on ball weight and protocol |
| Grip strength | Forearm and hand strength | 50–65+ kg per hand |
| Single-leg strength | Unilateral lower-body strength and stability | Strong, balanced left/right performance |

**Testing notes:** Record the surface, footwear, weather, timing method, and number of attempts. Use the same protocol each time so results can be compared fairly. For sprint tests, allow full recovery between maximal efforts. Throwing velocity should be measured after an appropriate warm-up and without encouraging unsafe maximum-effort throwing. Treat the example ranges as context, not pass/fail cut-offs.

## 2. Defensive measurements

| Measurement | What it assesses | Why it matters |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Outs Above Average (OAA) | Estimated outs made above average | Summarizes defensive value and range |
| First-step reaction | Initial movement after contact | Helps the infielder get to the ball sooner |
| Lateral range | Ability to move to either side and cover ground | Supports more plays on balls outside the player's immediate area |
| Route and footwork efficiency | Movement path, setup, and body control approaching the ball | Reduces wasted movement and supports a clean fielding position |
| Exchange time | Time needed to transfer the ball from glove to throwing hand | Supports quicker throws, especially on fast plays |
| Throwing velocity | Throw speed from relevant infield positions | Helps complete plays from deep or difficult positions |
| Throwing accuracy | Ability to make a well-directed, catchable throw | Helps prevent errors and keep runners from advancing |
| Double-play execution | Footwork, exchange, and throw quality during double-play attempts | Helps turn more opportunities into outs |
| Fielding errors / reliability | Consistency in completing routine plays | Routine chances must be converted consistently |

### Five key defensive skills

1. **First step and lateral quickness:** React quickly and move efficiently toward balls hit to either side.
2. **Fielding technique:** Stay balanced, get into a strong position, and secure the ball cleanly.
3. **Footwork and exchange:** Transition smoothly from fielding to throwing, including on double-play turns.
4. **Throwing strength and accuracy:** Make firm, catchable throws from different positions and distances.
5. **Agility and body control:** Decelerate, change direction, and recover balance while completing a play.

OAA is an advanced tracking metric. Coaches can also assess range and reliability with repeatable drills and video when Statcast data is unavailable.

## 3. Requirements by infield position

### Shortstop (SS)

Shortstop generally places the greatest emphasis on range, lateral quickness, and the ability to make difficult throws.

- Excellent first-step quickness and lateral range.
- Efficient footwork and reliable hands on balls hit in every direction.
- Strong, accurate throws from varied body positions and distances.
- Quick exchanges and dependable double-play execution.
- Clear communication and sound decisions on balls shared with teammates.

**Primary emphasis:** range, acceleration, agility, throwing strength, and reliable execution.

### Second base (2B)

Second base often places a premium on quick reactions, coordination, and efficient double-play turns.

- Quick first step, lateral movement, and body control.
- Reliable glove work on balls hit around the infield.
- Fast, coordinated exchanges and double-play footwork.
- Accurate throws, including from off-balance positions.
- Good communication with the shortstop and first baseman.

**Primary emphasis:** agility, coordination, quick exchanges, and double-play execution.

### Third base (3B)

Third base often places a premium on reaction speed and the ability to make strong throws after fielding hard-hit balls.

- Fast reactions and an explosive first step.
- Reliable hands on hard-hit balls and bunts.
- Strong, accurate throws across the infield.
- Ability to field and throw from varied body positions.
- Sound decisions on whether to charge, hold, or make a throw.

**Primary emphasis:** reaction speed, arm strength, reliable hands, and decisive execution.

### Important positional note

These are general tendencies, not rigid rules. Defensive alignment, ballpark conditions, team strategy, and an individual player's strengths can change the demands of each position. All infielders need reliable hands, sound decisions, clear communication, and accurate throws.

## 4. Suggested infielder evaluation scorecard

A coach can use the following example weighting to organize an assessment. It is a coaching framework, not an official MLB formula.

| Area | Example weight |
| --- | ---: |
| Defensive range and fielding reliability | 20% |
| Acceleration and lateral quickness | 15% |
| Throwing strength | 15% |
| Throwing accuracy and decisions | 15% |
| Footwork, exchange, and double-play execution | 15% |
| Reaction and agility | 10% |
| Hitting ability | 10% |
| **Total** | **100%** |

Score each area on a consistent scale (for example, 1–5), record the underlying test results, and reassess under the same conditions over time. Consider adjusting the weighting to suit the player's position and level.

## 5. Practical assessment checklist

- [ ] Record age, playing level, height, and body mass.
- [ ] Time a 10-yard sprint and a 60-yard dash using a consistent method.
- [ ] Record sprint speed from Statcast when available.
- [ ] Assess lateral movement and first-step reaction with repeatable drills.
- [ ] Measure throwing velocity with a radar gun.
- [ ] Assess throwing accuracy from relevant infield positions and body positions.
- [ ] Evaluate fielding reliability, footwork, exchanges, and double-play execution.
- [ ] Record exit velocity and bat speed if equipment is available.
- [ ] Review video from multiple angles where possible.
- [ ] Set position-specific goals and reassess periodically.

## 6. Interpreting the results

Do not evaluate an infielder from one number alone. A fast player may have inefficient routes or footwork; a player with a strong arm may struggle with accuracy; and a player with range may need to improve reliability on routine plays. Combine physical tests, defensive outcomes, video review, and game performance to build a balanced profile.
